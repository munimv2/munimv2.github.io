Self-hosted AI in a small law office: a confidentiality check against ABA Opinion 512 and state bar guidance

By MV2 of Munim, Inc. Written with AI agents working in his name. Every opinion, notice or statute cited below links to the issuing bar, court or legislature; links checked on 13 and 14 October 2026. This is technical information about software, not legal advice and not an ethics opinion.

Small firms run Ollama, LM Studio, Open WebUI or AnythingLLM on an office machine for one reason: client text never leaves the building. The bar opinions on generative AI reward that choice. Florida's Opinion 24-1 says informed consent under its Rule 4-1.6 is not needed when an in-house tool discloses nothing to a third party, and the Virginia State Bar's guidance says internally developed tools no one outside the firm can reach may protect confidential information. But the same opinions move the duty onto your setup. North Carolina's 2024 FEO 1 says the lawyer stays responsible for keeping information secure whether the tool is hosted by a vendor or runs on the firm's own servers. The ABA's Formal Opinion 512, Missouri's Informal Opinion 2026-09 and the Minnesota regulator's column all warn about disclosure to people inside the firm, where one client's information surfaces in another matter. So the questions a self-hosting office has to answer are technical ones. Here they are, with the command or setting that answers each.

California: from 1 January 2027 this is a statute

On 30 September 2026 California enacted SB 574 (Statutes of 2026, Chapter 858). Law-firm summaries of the new Business and Professions Code section 6068.1 describe its confidentiality test this way: an attorney may not enter confidential, personal identifying or other nonpublic information into a generative AI system unless access to it is limited to the attorney and authorised persons who are themselves bound to keep it confidential. The summaries say the test is about who can access what is entered, not about whether a product is public, enterprise or run by the firm. The same section requires checking AI output, including every citation, and disclosing generative AI use to the court.

Whether a particular office meets that test is a legal question for its lawyers. The facts it turns on, for a self-hosted setup, are technical, and they are the first three rows of the table further down: who on the network can reach the model server without logging in, who holds an account in the chat front end, and whether anything (sharing links, cloud model connections, web search, agents) sends text outside that circle. The State Bar's 2026 Practical Guidance adds that a tool which neither trains on nor shares inputs may still lack adequate security. MV2 read summaries of SB 574, not its enacted text, because the legislature's site refused automated access; read the statute itself before relying on any of this.

A local model does not learn from prompts, but the front end can keep them. List what the chat front end can call: Open WebUI tools and functions, AnythingLLM agent skills, MCP servers, connected mailboxes or drives. The bar opinions are advisory and differ by state; Colorado's and Rhode Island's rule changes, California's statute and the 2026 court rules of Connecticut, Florida, Indiana and New York are not. None of the 2026 court rules on filings says what a lawyer may put into an AI tool; they govern what comes out. The one state-court exception found so far is Vermont: its Supreme Court's Administrative Directive No. 38 says all court users must ensure that AI systems do not compromise sensitive or confidential information, without saying which systems qualify. Courts do not agree on what a "safe" AI system is

Some courts now say which kinds of AI system may receive confidential material, and they draw the line in different places. California's Rule of Court 10.430, Arkansas's Administrative Order No. 25 and the New York court system's interim policy go by who controls the system: a model the organisation runs itself counts as private. These three govern courts and their staff, not law firms. Pennsylvania's interim policy for court personnel goes by what the system does with what is entered: on its words, a system that retains user data is not secured, which would include a chat app that keeps history. Two federal courts' texts reach lawyers directly. Magistrate Judge Couvillier's standing order in the District of Nevada bars uploading sealed filings to an "open AI system", defined as one whose source code, learning materials and underlying models may be publicly accessible; on its words, an openly published model run on your own server is "open" and a proprietary cloud service is not. A June 2026 stipulated protective order in the same district (RMME v. Majestic Steel) is stricter: a system is "open" if its source code, training data or underlying models are public, so an openly published model can never count as its "closed" kind, however well it is secured. A District of Colorado protective-order ruling (Morgan v. V2X, March 2026) allows confidential material only into AI whose provider is contractually barred from storing or training on inputs, with the contract kept on file. Running the model on your own machine does not by itself settle any of these tests. The facts they turn on are who operates the system, who else can reach what is entered, whether inputs are kept or used for training, whether the model is openly published, what the provider has promised in writing and whether the court has approved the tool. MV2's side-by-side reading of eleven court texts quotes each one; how any of them applies to your office is a question for your lawyers and the court.

A ten-minute self check

  1. Run the free, read-only local-ai-checkup script on the machine that hosts the model. It reports what is exposed, risky or slow and prints nothing secret.
  2. From a second machine on the office network, try the curl line above. A refused connection is the right answer unless you share the server on purpose. Don't go by what a tool prints when it starts: KoboldCpp shows localhost while listening on every network.
  3. Put each tool's version into the CVE checker. If you run LiteLLM, check that it is 1.104.0 or later, or that a strong master key is set.
  4. Open the user list of your chat front end. Remove accounts you cannot name, turn off open sign-up, decide who is an admin, keep one workspace per matter, and turn off community sharing, memory features, tools and connectors you do not use. If you are installing Dify or Xinference, create the first admin (for Dify, or set INIT_PASSWORD) before anyone else can reach the server.
  5. Write the answers down with today's date.
