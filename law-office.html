Self-hosted AI in a small law office: a confidentiality check against ABA Opinion 512 and state bar guidance

By MV2 of Munim, Inc. Written with AI agents working in his name. Every opinion cited below links to the issuing bar or court; links checked on 13 and 14 October 2026. This is technical information about software, not legal advice and not an ethics opinion.

Small firms run Ollama, LM Studio, Open WebUI or AnythingLLM on an office machine for one reason: client text never leaves the building. The bar opinions on generative AI reward that choice. Florida's Opinion 24-1 says informed consent under its Rule 4-1.6 is not needed when an in-house tool discloses nothing to a third party, and the Virginia State Bar's guidance says internally developed tools that no one outside the firm can reach may protect confidential information. But the same opinions move the duty onto your setup. Virginia adds that the lawyer must still assess such a tool's security. North Carolina's 2024 FEO 1 says the lawyer stays responsible for keeping information secure whether the tool is hosted by a vendor or runs on the firm's own servers. The ABA's Formal Opinion 512 and Missouri's August 2026 Informal Opinion 2026-09 both point at people inside the firm: an AI tool drawing on one client's information while working for another. The director of Minnesota's lawyer-discipline office, writing on Opinion 512 in September 2024 in Bench & Bar of Minnesota, singled out exactly that point: client information can be improperly disclosed even with in-house-only tools. So the questions a self-hosting office has to answer are technical ones. Here they are, with the command or setting that answers each.

What the opinions ask, and what to check

What the guidance asksWhere it says soWhat to check on a self-hosted setup
Will the tool disclose client information to anyone outside the firm? ABA 512; DC Opinion 388 (provider must not see, keep or reuse it); Texas Opinion 705; Washington Advisory Opinion 202505; Michigan AI FAQs (client consent before protected information goes in, then confirm the provider's safeguards) Who can reach the model server. Ollama has no login of its own, so whatever can reach port 11434 can send prompts and read model names. From another machine on the network run curl http://<office-machine-ip>:11434/api/version; it should fail unless you meant to share it. On the machine itself, Ollama should listen on 127.0.0.1, not 0.0.0.0. How to check and fix.
Does the tool send anything home, keep inputs, or share by default? California Practical Guidance (review terms, retention and training practices); Oregon Formal Opinion 2025-205 (know how the tool stores information); Missouri Informal Opinion 2024-11 (vet terms, data security and how the provider uses inputs); Georgia Generative AI Toolkit (click-through terms may exclude any duty of confidentiality); Alabama Formal Opinion 2026-01 (know how the tool processes and keeps data; prompts and logs may be discoverable); NC 2024 FEO 1 Sharing, community features and chat history. Open WebUI ships with community sharing on; set ENABLE_COMMUNITY_SHARING=False unless you use it. Check whether your chat front end has web search, cloud model providers or a shared prompt library switched on; each is a path for client text to leave. Chat front ends also keep every prompt and answer by default, so decide how long chat history is kept and who can export it. Open WebUI settings.
Can people inside the firm read another matter's prompts, or can one client's material surface in another's work? ABA 512 (the intra-firm point); Missouri 2026-09 (one client's information used for another); Minnesota (the lawyer-discipline director's 2024 column on in-house-only tools); ABA 512 and Texas 705 on self-learning tools Users, workspaces and memory. A local model does not learn from prompts, but the front end can keep them, and a shared document library feeds every matter that uses it. List every account in Open WebUI or AnythingLLM, remove shared logins, keep one workspace or knowledge base per matter, and switch off memories or long-term context features unless each user understands they persist. AnythingLLM 1.13.0 up to 1.16.2 handed an owner's private Memories to anonymous visitors of an embedded chat widget (details).
Is the tool kept secure with reasonable efforts? NC 2024 FEO 1 (Rule 1.6(c)); Virginia guidance (in-house tools still need a security check; AI-enabled browsers can be steered by hidden text); Arizona State Bar guidance (encrypted, access-controlled platforms, checked with IT or security specialists); Illinois Supreme Court AI policy (AI must not compromise sensitive information); Pennsylvania Joint Formal Opinion 2024-200; NYC Bar Formal Opinion 2024-5; Alaska Ethics Opinion 2025-1 Known vulnerabilities, encryption and logins. Type your Ollama, Open WebUI, AnythingLLM, vLLM or LiteLLM version into the CVE checker. Several 2026 advisories for these tools have no CVE ID and are missing from the databases that dependency scanners read, so a scanner saying "clean" is not proof. Keep the machine's disk encrypted (BitLocker or FileVault), since chat history and uploaded documents sit in the front end's data folder, and keep the front end behind its own login. If the chat front end can browse the web or run tools, treat any page it reads as untrusted input.
Do you still paste into a cloud assistant sometimes? Florida 24-1 (informed consent before a third-party tool sees confidential information); DC 388; Washington 202505 (consumer products often lack confidentiality commitments); Ohio Ethics Guide on AI (anonymise client information whatever the tool); West Virginia L.E.O. 2024-01 Mask before you paste. Paste-Safe replaces names, organisations, emails and numbers with placeholders in your browser before text goes to ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, and restores them in the reply. Nothing is uploaded. It reduces risk; it does not anonymise.
Are citations verified before filing? Pennsylvania 2024-200; Oregon 2025-205; New Mexico Formal Opinion 2024-004; Kentucky KBA E-457; New Jersey Preliminary Guidelines; Illinois Supreme Court policy (review AI output before filing) Local models hallucinate too. The public database kept by Damien Charlotin listed 2,184 decisions involving hallucinated material on 10 October 2026 (CC BY 4.0). A smaller model on your own GPU is not more reliable than a cloud one, only more private. Check every citation against the court's own text or CourtListener before it leaves the office.
Is there a written policy, and do clients know? Missouri 2024-11 (written for a lawyer drafting a firm AI policy); New Jersey's 30 March 2026 Notice to the Bar (sample starter AI policy); Kentucky's sample AI use policy (April 2026); Illinois ARDC Guide to Implementing AI (sample office policy, client notice, consent form and terms-of-use checklist); New York State Bar Task Force report (sample engagement-letter clause) Write down what you checked. Which machine, which tools and versions, who has accounts, what listens on the network, how long chat history is kept, the date. A one-page record answers most of the questions above if anyone ever asks.

A ten-minute self check

  1. Run the free, read-only local-ai-checkup script on the machine that hosts the model. It reports what is exposed, risky or slow and prints nothing secret.
  2. From a second machine on the office network, try the curl line above. A refused connection is the right answer unless you share the server on purpose.
  3. Put each tool's version into the CVE checker.
  4. Open the user list of your chat front end. Remove accounts you cannot name. Turn off community sharing and memory features you do not use, and check that each matter's documents sit in their own workspace.
  5. Write the answers down with today's date.
